# Source types

Five types. Each one costs something different, proves something different, and lies in its own direction.

The rule that decides between them: **a source you cannot describe in one paragraph is not a source.** The paragraph has to name the source, the maker, the date, the coverage, the gap and the route in. If you cannot write it, you do not know what your number is, and the first journalist who asks will find that out.

Every section below ends with the sentence shape for that paragraph. Fill it in with your own facts, then paste it into the method note unchanged.

---

## 1. Public records

Registers, filings, licence lists, planning applications, court lists, freedom of information responses, published minutes.

| | |
|---|---|
| Access | A download, a search form, or a written request with a statutory response time |
| Cost | Time, sometimes a fee, sometimes a wait measured in weeks. A refused request can be appealed, which costs more time |
| What it can prove | That something is on the record, on a date, in the record's own words. This is the strongest of the five for "it is official" |
| The bias | The record exists for a reason other than your question. It shows compliance, not reality. What nobody filed is invisible, and absence of a record is not absence of the thing |

The trap: treating a register as a picture of the world. A register of licensed premises counts licensed premises, not premises that trade. Say "licensed", not "open".

**Method note sentence:** "The register of {what} published by {who} on {date}, covering {area} and holding {n} records. It records {what it records}, so a missing record means {what it means}, not that the activity does not happen."

---

## 2. A platform's own listing pages

A directory, a marketplace, a review site, a job board, an app store. You read the pages a buyer would read and copy what they say.

| | |
|---|---|
| Access | Reading pages by hand, or with a script if the terms allow it. Check the terms and the robots file before you automate anything |
| Cost | An hour per hundred short rows by hand. Re-checking when the page changes costs the same again |
| What it can prove | What the platform displayed on the day you looked, for the records you could see |
| The bias | Three layers. The platform decides who is listed. The ranking decides what you see first. The listing decides what it says, and a listing is marketing copy, not a description |

The trap: calling it a sample of the sector. It is a sample of one platform's sellers, filtered by that platform's onboarding and ranked by its algorithm. Say so.

The second trap: counting a page that personalises. If the order changes by visitor, record the order you saw and say it is your order.

**Method note sentence:** "The {n} listings on the public index of {platform}, copied with their published text and date on {date}. The index showed {total} records; {k} were {reason} and were excluded before coding. {Platform} chooses who is listed, so this is not a sample of {sector}."

---

## 3. An open dataset

A government statistical release, a published CSV, a research archive, an open register with an API.

| | |
|---|---|
| Access | A download, sometimes an API key, always a read of the schema and the notes |
| Cost | Almost nothing in money. Real time in reading the definitions, which is where the work is |
| What it can prove | Whatever the publisher measured, using the publisher's definitions, at the publisher's vintage |
| The bias | Someone else's categories, someone else's sample, someone else's date. A dataset that is two years old answers a question about two years ago |

The trap: quoting a figure without its definition. Two datasets can both count "small business" and disagree by a factor of three. Use the publisher's definition, quote it, and do not mix two publishers in one sentence.

The second trap: revision. Published figures get revised. Record the version or the download date, or your number will stop matching the source's own page.

**Method note sentence:** "{Publisher}'s {dataset name}, version or release of {date}, table {reference}, covering {population} for {period}. The definition of {term} is the publisher's: {definition}. Figures are as published on {date} and may be revised."

---

## 4. A survey you run

You write the questions, you field them, you own the sample.

| | |
|---|---|
| Access | A panel provider, a trade body's list, or your own audience. Each has a different meaning |
| Cost | Money for a panel, or weeks of chasing for a small list. Cheap surveys are cheap because the sample is whoever answered |
| What it can prove | What people say about themselves, on the day they answered, in response to your wording |
| The bias | Self-selection, question wording, and the order of the questions. An audience survey measures your audience |

The trap: reporting an audience survey as if it were the population. "83% of our users" is a fact about your users. It becomes a claim about the market the moment you drop the words "our users", and that is the moment it becomes wrong.

The second trap: leading wording. "How concerned are you about hidden delivery costs?" has an answer built in. Ask "what, if anything, do you check before ordering?" and count what comes back.

**Method note sentence:** "A survey of {n} {who}, fielded {dates} via {route}. Respondents were {how recruited}. The sample is {what it is a sample of}, and it is not weighted to {population}."

---

## 5. A sample you collect

You define a frame, read every row in it, and record what you see. This is the type the worked example imitates, and the type this method is built around.

| | |
|---|---|
| Access | You read. Browser, PDF, shop window, printed programme |
| Cost | About an hour per hundred short rows, plus a second pass when the rule turns out to be vague |
| What it can prove | What is written in the rows you read, against a rule you published |
| The bias | Your frame chose the rows, and your attention coded them. Both are visible if you publish the rule and the sample |

The trap: collecting until the pattern appears. Decide the frame and the size before you start, and record the population before filtering.

The second trap: coding from memory. A row you read an hour ago is not the row any more. Code from the text in front of you, in the sheet, one row at a time.

**Method note sentence:** "The {n} {what} in {frame}, read and recorded on {date}. The frame held {total} records; {k} were {reason}. Each row was coded against the rule in this document by one coder, and a fixed sample of {m} was re-read as a check."

## Choosing between them

| If you need | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| A number that cannot be argued with | Public records | The record is the authority, not you |
| A number about a market you can see | A platform's pages | It is the market's own copy, and it is cheap |
| A number that covers a whole country | An open dataset | You cannot read a country by hand |
| A number about attitudes | A survey | Nothing else reaches what people think |
| A number about a practice nobody records | A sample you collect | Someone has to read the rows |

Two sources are better than one when they measure the same thing differently. They are worse than one when they measure different things and you put them in the same sentence.

## Sources that are not sources

Each of these has appeared in a real pack. None is citable.

| Not a source | Why | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "Various online sources" | It cannot be checked, repeated or challenged | Name one, or drop the claim |
| A chart in someone else's deck | The chart may be right; you cannot see the data | Find the underlying release, or do not use the figure |
| A summary written by an AI assistant | It may be accurate and it is not evidence | Go to the document it summarised |
| A figure with no date | It may be five years old | Find the release, quote its period |
| A screenshot of a dashboard | You cannot see the query behind it | Ask for the export, or reproduce the query |
| A paywalled report you did not read | The abstract is not the finding | Buy it, or cite the abstract as an abstract |
| Your own analytics, described as market data | It is your audience | Say "our users", every time |

## Permission, terms and personal data

Four checks before you collect, in this order, because each one can stop the project.

1. **The terms of the site.** Many allow reading and forbid automated collection. Read the terms and the robots file. If automation is forbidden, collect by hand, or pick another source.
2. **The licence of the dataset.** Open means open under a named licence. Record which one, because it decides whether you can republish rows.
3. **Personal data.** Names, addresses, phone numbers, health information and anything that identifies a private individual are not rows to publish. Count them if you must, and publish the count, not the people.
4. **The public interest test.** If the study would let a reader identify a named individual's private circumstances, the answer is no, whatever the terms allow.

A study that cannot be published safely is not a study. It is a liability with a chart.
